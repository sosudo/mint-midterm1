Question: ? $\rho(E) = \ms(E)$ if $E\in\mathcal E$?

Proposition 2.9 $\ms(E) = \rho(E)$ $\forall E\in\mathcal E$ iff $\rho$ is countably subadditive i.e. $\rho(E)\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\rho(E_n)$ whenever $E, E_n\in\mathcal E$ and $E\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$.

[$\Leftarrow$] Assume that $\rho$ is countably subadditive. If $E\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$, $n\in\N$ then $$\rho(E)\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\rho(E_n).$$ Inf on RHS over all such coverings, get $\rho(E)\le\ms(E)$.

$\rho(E)$ is a lower bound for the cost of every admissible covering, hence $\le$ the greatest lower bound, $\ms(E)$.

Thus + Prop 2.7 $\Rightarrow\rho(E) = \ms(E)$.

[$\Rightarrow$] $E, E_n\in\mathcal E$, $E\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n$. $$\underbrace{\rho(E) = \ms(E)}_{\text{hyp}}\ \underbrace{\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\rho(E_n)}_{\text{def of }\ms}$$

The equality is the hypothesis applied to $E\in\mathcal E$. The inequality holds because $\{E_n\}$ is one admissible covering of $E$ and $\ms(E)$ is the infimum over all of them. Reading the two ends together: $\rho(E)\le\sum\rho(E_n)$, which is countable subadditivity.

The outer measure of a rectangle

$(\mathcal L^N)^*(R) = \ell(R)$ $\forall R$ rectangle

Always (Prop 2.7) $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)\le\rho(R)$.

step 1 claim that $$(\mathcal L^N)^*(R) = \inf\Big\{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ell(R_n) : R\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty R_n,\ R_n \text{ open}\Big\} = \alpha(R)$$

$(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)\le\alpha(R)$ clearly because we are restricting the admissible sets on RHS

Every cover by open rectangles is a cover by rectangles, so the infimum defining $\alpha(R)$ runs over a subset of the covers allowed for $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)$; an infimum over fewer things is larger.

Fix $\eps\gt0$, let $R_n$ rectangle (not nec. open) $$\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ell(R_n)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R) + \eps,\qquad R\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty R_n$$

Definition of infimum: if $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)\lt\infty$, the number $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+\eps$ is not a lower bound for the costs of covers, so some cover costs at most that. (If $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R) = \infty$ — impossible here since it's $\le\ell(R)\lt\infty$ — there would be nothing to prove.)

$\forall n\in\N$ Dilate slightly each $R_n$: $J_n\supset R_n$, $J_n$ open, $\ell(J_n)\le\ell(R_n) + \dfrac{\eps}{2^n}$

Say $R_n = \prod_{i=1}^N I_i$ with $I_i$ having endpoints $a_i\le b_i$. Let $J_n = \prod_{i=1}^N(a_i - t, b_i + t)$, an open rectangle containing $R_n$. Its volume $\prod_i(b_i - a_i + 2t)$ is a polynomial in $t$, hence continuous, and equals $\ell(R_n)$ at $t = 0$. So for $t\gt0$ small enough, $\ell(J_n)\le\ell(R_n)+\eps/2^n$. (Works even if $R_n$ is degenerate, e.g. a point.)

Then $R\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty J_n$

$$(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ell(J_n)\le\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ell(R_n) + \eps\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R) + 2\eps$$

What this line really bounds is $\alpha(R)$ (that's the inequality we still need): $\{J_n\}$ is a cover of $R$ by open rectangles ($R\subseteq\bigcup R_n\subseteq\bigcup J_n$), so $\alpha(R)\le\sum\ell(J_n)$. Then $\sum\ell(J_n)\le\sum(\ell(R_n)+\eps/2^n) = \sum\ell(R_n)+\eps$, and finally use the choice of $\{R_n\}$. So $\alpha(R)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+2\eps$.

Let $\eps\to0^+$.

$\alpha(R)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+2\eps$ for all $\eps\gt0$ gives $\alpha(R)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)$; with the first inequality, equality.

Step 2 $R$ is a closed rectangle. claim $\ell(R)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)$

Fix $\eps\gt0$. Step 1: $\exists R_n$ open, $R\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty R_n$, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ell(R_n)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+\eps$.

By step 1, $(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)$ is also the infimum over covers by open rectangles, so we can pick an open cover whose cost is within $\eps$ of it.

$R$ compact $\Rightarrow\exists n\in\N$: $R\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^n R_i$.

A closed rectangle is closed and bounded in $\R^N$, hence compact (Heine–Borel). The $R_n$ form an open cover, so finitely many suffice; after relabelling, $R_1,\dots,R_n$. This is the crucial step where countable becomes finite — and why we needed open rectangles in step 1.

Extend the faces of each $R_n$ to hyperplanes, in order to get a finite partition of $R$ … subrectangles $J_k$

Each $R_i$ has, in each coordinate direction, two endpoint values. Collect all these values (and those of $R$) in coordinate $j$ and sort them: they cut the $j$-th side of $R$ into intervals. Products of these intervals cut $R$ into finitely many non-overlapping subrectangles $J_1,\dots,J_k$ (a grid). Each $J_m$ lies entirely inside every $R_i$ it meets (no face of $R_i$ crosses it), and since the $R_i$ cover $R$, each $J_m$ lies inside at least one $R_i$. See the interactive figure below.

$$\ell(R) = \sum_{k=1}^k\ell(J_k)\le\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(R_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^\infty\ell(R_i)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+\eps$$

Equality: volume is additive over a grid partition: $R = \prod[a_j,b_j]$ with each side cut into pieces; $\prod_j(b_j-a_j) = \prod_j\sum_{\text{pieces}}(\text{piece length}) = \sum_{\text{grid cells}}\prod_j(\text{lengths})$ by expanding the product. (The $J$'s overlap only on faces, which have zero length in some direction.)

First $\le$: group the $J$'s by an $R_i$ containing them. The grid cells inside a given $R_i$ are part of the analogous grid of $R_i$, so their volumes add up to at most $\ell(R_i)$ (by the same expansion). Every $J$ is counted in at least one group.

Second $\le$: adding more non-negative terms. Third: choice of the cover.

let $\eps\to0^+$ get $\ell(R)\le(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)$

Step 3 $R$ arbitrary

Fix $\eps\gt0$, find $J\subseteq R$ closed rectangle, $\ell(R)\le\ell(J)+\eps$.

If $R = \prod I_i$ with endpoints $a_i\lt b_i$, take $J = \prod[a_i+t, b_i-t]$ for small $t\gt0$: it's closed, inside $R$, and its volume $\prod(b_i-a_i-2t)\to\ell(R)$ as $t\to0$. (If some side of $R$ is degenerate, $\ell(R) = 0$ and there's nothing to prove.)

$$\ell(R)\le\ell(J)+\eps\underset{\text{(Step 2)}}{\le}(\mathcal L^N)^*(J)+\eps\underset{\text{(monotonicity)}}{\le}(\mathcal L^N)^*(R)+\eps.$$

Step 2 applies to $J$ because $J$ is closed. Monotonicity of the outer measure (Def 2.6 (ii)) applies because $J\subseteq R$.
